About the project

A new map of an old river

In 2022 and 2023, aircraft measured every square meter of the Upper Mad, 334 km² of mountains above Ruth Lake, with laser pulses that pass through the trees to the ground.

We used that lidar to map what nobody had mapped all at once: every road, every place a road crosses a stream, the valley floors where the river could spread out again, and the forest the 2020 August Complex left standing. It burned 95% of the basin.

Ruth Lake, behind Matthews Dam, stores water for the Humboldt Bay Municipal Water District. What washes off these slopes ends up there. That makes this watershed everyone's business, including people who will never drive its roads.

This is the first piece of a larger assessment. The same toolkit will next cover the whole Mad River basin, down to the ocean.

Four things the lidar shows

It sees the ground

Laser pulses slip between leaves. The ones that reach the soil draw the bare-earth surface, so old roads and stream channels show up under the forest.

Roads move water

When a culvert plugs, a creek can run down the road instead of across it and cut a new gully. We checked every crossing for that risk.

Height above the river

Subtract the river's level from the terrain and the valley floor lights up. Ground within 2 m of the water is where low-tech fixes like beaver dam analogs can reconnect it.

Islands of forest

The 2020 fire left patches of living forest. Some sit near homes and roads, where thinning protects people; tall old stands are refugia to keep.

Where this is going

From the Upper Mad to the whole basin

The Upper Mad is the pilot. The same lidar toolkit, the same measures and this same explorer will grow to the whole Mad River basin, 1,281 km² from the headwaters to the Pacific, so every creek can be compared with every other.

Areas are the USGS HUC10 watersheds; the pilot's lidar covers the Upper Mad plus its rim (334 km²). The order of the later phases is a planning placeholder.

Screening, not survey. Everything here comes from lidar and public data. None of it has been checked on the ground. It shows where to look first. Private land is summarized by watershed and is not a finding about any one property.
Proposed · how the data would be run

One home for the basin's data

A basin-wide assessment makes a lot of data: lidar products for every subwatershed, tens of thousands of crossings, and what residents and partners add. This proposal puts it in one place the Watershed Research and Training Center would steward: the assessment layers as open files in a public repository, community input in a review sheet in WRTC's Google Drive, and this explorer as the public face.

Owned and open

The layers are open files (GeoJSON, GeoPackage) in WRTC's own repository, and the site runs on free static hosting. No paid platform and no software licenses.

Reviewed

Every entry arrives in the review sheet as pending. Nothing shows until a reviewer marks it approved, so sensitive places stay off the public map. No contact details are collected.

Repeatable

Each subwatershed goes through the same pipeline and lands in the same tables, so the Middle and Lower Mad drop in beside the Upper Mad.

    Where these prices come from

    North Coast road projects have run about $40,000 to $100,000 per mile of decommissioned road (Redwood Creek, 2011; Mt. Baker-Snoqualmie NF), and about $64,000 per mile across the North Coast Coho Project's 151 miles of road treatment. Full recontouring in Redwood National Park runs $80,000 to $400,000 per mile. Crossing excavation is set from PWA projects at $13 to $29 per cubic yard of sediment saved (about $17 to $38 per m³). Dips ran $390 to $1,200 each in Forest Service Region 5 restoration contracts; a critical dip is deeper and armored. Road assessments ran $3,685 to $4,800 per mile in six North Coast proposals from 2020 to 2026 (North Coast Water Board). The culvert price is an estimate for a forest-road pipe with excavation and rock; fish-passage structures cost far more. All are planning figures, not bids.
